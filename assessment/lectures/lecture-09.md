# Lecture 09 Assessment

## Source
`lectures/lecture-09.tex`

## Scope
This lecture surveys the established SUSY duality landscape: Kutasov--Schwimmer duality, the orthogonal and symplectic cases, s-confinement, product-group dualities, and the Klebanov--Strassler cascade. It is the broadest theory survey in the course.

## Strengths
The breadth is impressive and the lecture does a good job of showing that Seiberg duality is not an isolated trick. The k-fold generalisation, the classical-group dualities, and the s-confining boundary cases all reinforce the idea that duality is a structural phenomenon. The explicit anomaly-matching calculations for SO and Sp are especially valuable because they make the abstract generality feel earned rather than asserted.

The synthesis section is also effective. By collecting the dualities into one comparison table and then returning to the DSM relevance, the lecture avoids becoming a disconnected catalog. The reader gets both the landscape and the course-specific takeaway.

## Concerns
- The lecture sometimes reads like a well-curated list of known results rather than a single argument. That is not a flaw in itself, but it does reduce the sense of progression.
- The bridge back to the DSM is present, but the relevance of the non-SU cases to the course’s main line could be stated more sharply. Otherwise they risk feeling like interesting detours.
- Some sections, especially on product groups and cascades, introduce heavy structural ideas quickly. Readers may need more signposting about which parts are essential for later lectures and which are background.

## Suggestions
- Add a short “why this family matters” sentence at the start or end of each duality block so the reader can keep track of the logic.
- Make the final relevance paragraph more explicit about which duality ingredients will reappear in Lecture 10 and which are just part of the broader landscape.
- If space allows, distinguish “canonical theorem,” “boundary case,” and “phenomenological analogy” more clearly across the lecture. That would strengthen the conceptual hierarchy of the survey.
